# Death by slides

Make slides that help a live audience follow the speaker. The five rules come from David JP Phillips's talk ["How to Avoid Death by PowerPoint"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Iwpi1Lm6dFo) (TEDxStockholmSalon, 2014), which bases slide design on how attention and working memory behave. See [Credit](#credit) at the end.

The idea behind every rule: **the speaker is the presentation, and the slides are the visual aid.** People cannot read and listen at the same time, and they hold very little in mind at once. A slide that asks them to read, count, or search takes their attention from the speaker. So each slide carries one idea, in a form the eye takes in at a glance, and the speaker supplies the rest.

Use this skill with whatever tool builds the deck. It changes what goes on the slides, not which program makes them.

## The default slide to avoid

Left alone, most people and most AI agents produce the same slide: a large headline, four to seven bullets written as full sentences, a logo, a footer, and a page number, on a white background. Phillips shows this exact layout and puts it with the bad examples. It breaks all five rules at once. When your draft looks like that, start the slide over.

## First, find out how the deck will be used

These rules are for slides shown while someone talks. Ask, or infer from the request:

- **Presented live** (in a room or on a call). Apply every rule in full.
- **Sent to be read alone** (a pre-read, a report in slide form, a deck with no speaker). No voice competes with the text, so sentences are fine. Still keep one message per slide, make the main thing the largest, and use contrast to guide the eye. Say that you treated it as a reading deck.
- **Both.** Build the live deck, and put the full wording in the speaker notes or a separate handout. Do not make one deck do both jobs. That compromise is how slides fill up with text.

## The five rules

### 1. One message per slide

Each slide makes one point. If a slide covers two topics, split it into two slides.

**Why:** attention goes to one thing at a time. With two messages on screen, part of the audience reads one while the speaker talks about the other, and they lose both.

**Test:** say what the slide is for in one short sentence. If you need the word "and", split it.

### 2. No sentences on the slide

Put short phrases on the slide, a few words each, with an image when one helps. Put everything the speaker will say in the speaker notes.

**Why:** this is the redundancy effect, from research by John Sweller and Richard Mayer. When the audience reads sentences while the speaker talks, the two streams of words collide and little of either is remembered. Phillips writes it as 1 + 1 = 0.

**How:**
- Cut each bullet to its key words. "The exterior delivers comfort through flat free tires and speed through aerodynamics" becomes "Flat free tires" and "Aerodynamic".
- Move the full sentence to the speaker notes. Every slide gets notes, so nothing is lost.
- A quote or definition the audience needs to read in full is the exception. Give it a slide of its own and write "pause and let them read" in the notes.

### 3. Make the most important thing the largest

The eye goes to the largest object first. On most slides the content matters more than the headline, so the content should be larger than the headline.

**Why:** standard templates make the title the largest text and the content the smallest. The audience then spends most of its time on the title, which is the least useful part.

**How:**
- Make the headline small, or drop it when the content says it already.
- When the slide is a single number or phrase, make it large, but keep all of it on the slide with a clear margin on every side. A wide item such as "9% → 23%" needs a smaller size than a short one such as "$60k". Set the size from the width of the whole line, not from the height of the slide.
- Within a list or chart, the item being discussed is the largest or the brightest.

### 4. Use contrast to steer the eye, on a dark background

The eye is also pulled to whatever stands out from its surroundings. Use that to show the audience where to look.

**How:**
- **Dark background, light text.** A white slide is the brightest and largest thing in the room, so it beats the speaker for attention. A dark slide lets people look at the speaker. Use a light background only when the person asks for one, their brand requires it, or the deck will be printed.
- **Dim what you are not talking about.** In a list, show the current item at full brightness and the others in a dim grey. In a table or chart, do the same with the one row, column, or bar that matters. This is how a slide can hold a large table and still be clear.
- **Keep text easy to read.** Light text on a light background, or dark on dark, is the most common contrast mistake.

### 5. Six objects or fewer

Count everything the eye can land on: the headline, each bullet, each image, each chart, the logo, the footer, the page number. Keep the total at six or fewer.

**Why:** people take in up to about five or six objects at a glance. Past that they have to count and search, which is slower and costs more effort. Phillips puts the cost at about five times as much. An audience will not pay that cost for a slide. They stop looking.

**How:**
- Remove page numbers, footers, dates, and "confidential" lines. They add objects and no meaning. "Page 12 of 95" tells the audience how much is left to sit through.
- Put the logo on the first and last slide only.
- When a slide is still over six, split it. Do not shrink the text to fit.
- A chart counts as one object when it shows one clear pattern. If the audience has to read many labels to get the point, simplify the chart or dim all but the part that matters.

## More slides is fine

Following these rules makes the deck longer. Phillips turned a 95-slide deck into 135 slides and it worked better. The number of slides was never the problem. The number of objects on each slide is.

A slide can stay on screen for ten seconds. If the person has a slide limit, tell them that fitting the same content into fewer slides makes each one worse, and offer to cut content instead.

## How to build a deck

1. **Get the message.** Ask who the audience is, what they should do or believe afterward, and how long the talk is. Write the whole talk's point in one sentence.
2. **Outline as messages.** List the points in order, one line each. Each line becomes one slide. This is the step where the deck is made clear, before any design.
3. **Write the speaker notes first.** For each slide, write what the speaker says. This gives the sentences a home so they stay off the slides.
4. **Then put on the slide only what helps the audience see the point.** A few words, one number, one image, or one chart. Some points need no slide at all. A black slide while the speaker tells a story is a good choice.
5. **Add structure.**
   - Section slides: a number and one or two words ("2. Pricing").
   - Builds: when a slide has several parts, reveal them one at a time as the speaker reaches each, and dim the earlier ones.
   - A running example: fixing or extending one example across the talk is easier to follow than a new example each time.
6. **End with recall, not a recap list.** Ask the audience a question for each main point and let them answer, or show one word per point. A closing slide of summary bullets breaks rule 2 at the moment people are most likely to remember.
7. **Check every slide** with the checklist below before you show the deck.
8. **Look at the result.** Large text is the most likely thing to run off the edge of a slide, and a cut off number is worse than a small one. If you can render the deck or take a screenshot, do it and look at every slide. Fix any slide where content touches or passes an edge by making that content smaller. In a web page deck, size large text so it shrinks with the screen and never wraps or overflows. If you cannot see the result, say so and ask the person to check the slides with the largest text.

## Checklist for every slide

Run this on each slide and fix what fails. Do not hand over a deck with known failures unless the person chose them.

- [ ] One message. You can state it without "and".
- [ ] No full sentences, except a quote slide. Each text item is about six words or fewer.
- [ ] Six objects or fewer, counting the headline, logo, and footer.
- [ ] The most important element is the largest. The headline is not.
- [ ] Dark background with light text, or a stated reason for light.
- [ ] One clear place for the eye to go first. Everything else is dimmer or smaller.
- [ ] No page number, footer, or repeated logo.
- [ ] Everything fits inside the slide. Large numbers and long words are the usual cause of text cut off at the edges. If you can render the slide, look at it. If you cannot, size the largest element to the slide width.
- [ ] Speaker notes hold what the speaker will say.

## Working on a deck that already exists

When the person gives you a deck they already made, change nothing until they approve. These rules change a deck a lot: the background goes dark, most of the text leaves the slides, and ten slides can become twenty five. A person who opens their deck and finds that with no warning will think their work was thrown away. Show the plan first, give the reason for each change, and let them decide.

This applies even when the request is "fix my slides" or "optimize my deck". Asking for a fix is not approval of each change.

### 1. Read the deck and change nothing

Read every slide. For each one, count the objects and note which rules it breaks.

### 2. Show the plan

Tell the person what will change before you touch the file. Use this order:

1. **What stays the same.** Say plainly that their content, their points, and the order are kept. Text that comes off a slide moves into the speaker notes. Nothing is deleted.
2. **Changes to the whole deck**, each with its reason in one line. For example:
   - "Background changes from white to dark. A white slide is the brightest thing in the room and pulls eyes off you."
   - "Sentences move into the speaker notes. People cannot read and listen at the same time."
   - "Page numbers and footers are removed. They add objects and no meaning."
   - "The deck grows from 10 slides to about 22. Each slide gets one message, so crowded slides are split."
3. **Changes to each slide**, in a table:

   | Slide | Now | What changes | Why |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | 3 | Headline, 6 sentence bullets, logo, footer (10 objects) | Split into 3 slides, a few words each. Sentences go to notes. | Two messages on one slide, and more than six objects |
   | 4 | One chart, small title | No change | Already clear |

   List slides that need no change too, so the person can see you checked them.

Keep the reasons short and in plain words. The person should finish reading the plan and know what their deck will look like.

### 3. Ask how they want to go through it

Ask one question and wait for the answer:

- **One slide at a time.** You show each change and they approve it before you make it.
- **All at once.** They approve the whole plan and you make every change in one pass.

They can also stop at the plan and make the changes themselves.

### 4. Make only the approved changes

**One slide at a time:**
- Get approval for the changes to the whole deck first, since they touch every slide.
- Then take the slides in order. For each one, show what it has now, what it will become (the words on the slide and the text going to notes), and the reason. Ask: approve, skip, or change it.
- Make the change only after they approve it. If they skip a slide, leave it as it is.
- They can switch at any point by saying "do the rest". Then finish the remaining slides in one pass.

**All at once:**
- Ask for a yes on the whole plan. They can take items out first, such as "keep the white background" or "leave slide 7 alone".
- Make every approved change in one pass.

In both modes:
- Work on a copy and keep the original file, so they can go back. Tell them where the original is.
- Keep their content and order. Split slides instead of deleting points, and move cut text into speaker notes.
- Do not add a change that was not in the plan. If you find a new problem partway through, say so and ask.

### 5. Report what you did

List the slides you changed, the slides you skipped at their request, and any slide that still breaks a rule and why.

### If the person says not to ask

If they say "just do it, don't ask me", skip the questions. Still show the plan from step 2 before you start and keep the original file, so they know what changed and can undo it.

## When the person asks for something that breaks a rule

Do what they ask. It is their deck. Say once, in one line, what the rule is and what it costs to break it, then build it their way. Common cases:

- **A required company template with a white background, logo, and footer.** Use it. Apply the other rules inside it: one message, few words, six objects of content, large content.
- **"Put all the details on the slide."** Offer the details in speaker notes or a handout. If they still want them on the slide, do it.
- **A fixed slide count.** Keep to it by cutting points, not by filling slides.

## Output

Give the person:

1. The deck, in the format they asked for.
2. Speaker notes for every slide, inside the deck when the format supports notes, otherwise as a separate list.
3. A short line on choices you made, such as "Dark background, 24 slides for a 15 minute talk, full wording is in the notes." Mention any slide that still breaks a rule and why.

## Example: one slide, fixed

**Before (16 objects, two messages, sentences, white background):**

```
Supercar concept                                   [large headline]

Facts about the Classic car
Versatility needs speed. That's why the Classic Car offers a maximum
load capacity of 850 litres, with room for two golf bags...

Exterior advantages of the Classic Car
The exterior combines the dynamic road-hugging focus of a coupe,
delivering comfort in flat free tires, speed by its aerodynamics...

Copyright NO Corporation      Version 2.1      Page 12/95
```

**After (two slides, each with five objects, dark background):**

```
Slide A                              Slide B
Supercar exterior  [small]           Supercar facts  [small]

[photo of the car]                   850 litres  [very large]
Flat free tires
Xenon lights                         Notes: "Versatility needs speed.
New material                         The load space takes two golf
Aerodynamic                          bags..."

Notes: "The exterior combines
the road-hugging focus of a
coupe..."
```

On slide A, each phrase lights up as the speaker reaches it and the others dim.
